# Build-to-Teach Framework

## What it is

The EM lays out the curriculum and training plan before anything starts — what topics matter, what order to tackle them in, and the schedule. The engineer's job is to work through that plan by building something real, and to write up what they did in a way the next engineer could follow on their own.

So two things come out of every cycle: a working system (the capstone), and a module explaining how to build it. Both get built by the same person, at the same time, not one after the other. This isn't a new idea — it's how the capstone already works. What's different here is that the EM stays involved the whole way through, not just at the final review.

## Who does what

The EM sets the curriculum (topics, sequence, stack constraints — Data, Cloud, DevOps/Platform, or Software, depending on the track) and the training plan (schedule, stages, what "done" means at each one). They also write the capstone brief and keep it lined up with the existing rubric. As the engineer works, the EM reviews drafts along the way, not just the finished product, and decides what's worth keeping for the next cohort.

The engineer builds the capstone and writes up each stage as they finish it — not as an afterthought once the system already works.

## How a cycle runs

1. EM hands over the curriculum, training plan, capstone brief, and rubric.  
2. Engineer builds, writing up each stage as they go.  
3. At each stage, EM reviews both the system and the write-up, and pushes for changes before moving on.  
4. Engineer revises based on that feedback.  
5. The finished pair — system and training module — goes into the content library, and the EM decides what carries into the next cohort's plan.

## Why review as you go, not at the end

Write-ups done after a system already works tend to be thin — there's no real pressure to make them good once the thing runs. Reviewing both pieces together, stage by stage, means the EM is judging whether the work is teachable, not just whether it works. It also catches problems early instead of finding them in a finished draft nobody wants to redo.

## How this fits with what's already running

Nothing about the tracks, stack requirements, or rubric changes. This is just the mechanism that makes "capstones feed the next cohort" actually happen — a checkpoint at a time, instead of hoping it comes together at handoff.

## Still open

- How often should checkpoints happen — weekly, or once per stage? Real tradeoff against keeping EM overhead flat.  
- Who owns the content library over time — the EM each cohort, or someone who rotates through it?  
- Should the EM's own review notes get folded into the training material, or stay private feedback?
